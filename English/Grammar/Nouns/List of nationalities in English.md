# Nationalities

#### When we refer to a nation or region, we can use:
1. the name of the country or region: **Turkey, Japan, Germany, Brazil, Asia**
2. a singular noun that we use for a person from the country or region: **a Turk, a Japanese, a German, a Brazilian, an Asian**
3. the plural expression the ... sued for the whole population of a country or region: **the Turks, the Japanese, the Germans, the Brazilians, the Asians**
4. an adjective: **Turkish, Japanese, German, Brazilian, Asian**

#### The name of nation language is commonly the same as the national adjective.

In this case, the words are nouns and may be modified by adjectives.

#### We don't use "the" or the word "language"

> Do you  speak Chinese?
> Not: ~~Do you speak the Chinese?~~ or ~~Dou you speak Chinese language?~~
> Russian is difficult to learn, isn't it, especially the alphabet?
> She speaks fluent French

#### We use a capital letter when we refer to nationality, a language, a country, and a region:

> They have studied American literature.
> Not: ~~They have studied american literature~~

> **Country**: I live in **England**.
> **Adjective**: He reads **English** literature.
> **Noun**: She is **Englishwoman**.

Note: We use **the + nationality adjective ending** in **-ese** or **-ish** with a plural verb, to refer to all people of that nationality:

> **The Chinese** are very hard-working.
> **The Spanish** often go to sleep in the afternoon.

When we talk, about the United Kingdom (UK) English is not the same as British. English is not used for Scottish or Welsh or Northern Irish people. (Great) Britain refers to the territory of England, Scotland and Wales. The United Kingdom refers to England, Scotland, Wales and the six countries of Northern Ireland. Some people from Northern Ireland refer to themselves as British in the context of the United Kingdom of Britain and North Ireland. However everyone from Northern Ireland has the right to Irish nationality and can hold an Irish passport. Irish also refers to citizens of the Irish Republic.

The Scots themselves prefer the adjective Scots and it also occurs in the compounds Scotsman and Scotswoman. We use the adjective Scotch to refer only to food and drink from Scotland e.g. Scotch (broth is a kind of soup).

Nowadays we use the noun Briton only to refer to the ancient tribes that lived in Britain:

> The antient Britons built huge earth works to bury their kinds and leaders

The short form of British, Brit, is often as a noun (or less commonly as an adjective) in journalistic style and in informal situations to refer to British people:

> The British have a bad reputation in some countries.

## Part 2. Languages and Articles (7-10):

**7.** Choose the correct sentence:  
a) Do you speak the Chinese?  
b) Do you speak Chinese language?  
c) Do you speak Chinese?  
d) Do you speak the Chinese language?
> [!NOTE]- Question 7
> **c) Do you speak Chinese?**  
> *With language names, do not use the article **the** or the word **language**.*

**8.** How do you correctly say "Russian is difficult"?  
a) Russian is difficult to learn.  
b) The Russian is difficult to learn.  
c) Russian language is difficult to learn.  
d) Russia is difficult to learn.
> [!NOTE]- Question 8
> **a) Russian is difficult to learn.**  
> *Language alone: **Russian** without article, not **the Russian**.*

**9.** Is it correct to say _"She speaks the French"_?  
a) Yes, it's correct  
b) No, it should be _She speaks French_  
c) No, it should be _She speaks the French language_  
d) Yes, if you mean the French language
> [!NOTE]- Question 9
> **b) No, it should be *She speaks French***  
> *The article **the** is not used before language names when they function as languages.*

**10.** Which sentence is grammatically correct?  
a) Turkish is spoken in Turkey.  
b) The Turkish is spoken in Turkey.  
c) The Turkish language spoken in Turkey.  
d) Turkey speaks Turkish language.
> [!NOTE]- Question 10
> **a) Turkish is spoken in Turkey.**  
> *Language names without article; passive construction is correct.*
